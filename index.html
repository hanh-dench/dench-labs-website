CRU — Canonical Representation Unit

For decades, digital representations have depended on the systems around them—databases, platforms, repositories, networks, and authorities.

CRU explores a different possibility:

What if the representation itself could become the trusted primitive?

A CRU is designed to create a portable, verifiable digital representation that can remain meaningful across systems and environments—without being defined solely by where it is stored, who hosts it, or which system recognizes it.

Portable. Verifiable. Independent by design.

A new primitive for a distributed world.

Dench Labs is exploring what becomes possible when digital representations can stand on their own.

03 / ZTA VS CRU

Zero Trust secures access. CRU governs capability.

CRU does not reject Zero Trust. Zero Trust establishes powerful security principles; CRU provides a computational architectural primitive in which those principles can become intrinsic to the units performing work.

ZERO TRUST ARCHITECTURE
Identity → Policy → Continuous Verification → Access / Resource

Identity, device posture, context, policy, monitoring, least privilege, and micro-segmentation determine whether access should be granted and maintained.

CRU ARCHITECTURE
Canonical Representation → Integrity → Persistence → Capability Composition → Interoperability

Within the CRU, identity, authorization, execution, state, and governance become coupled to the representation and its capabilities.

Continuous monitoring

ZTA continuously evaluates trust and access conditions. CRU extends this into the lifecycle of the capability itself.

Least privilege

ZTA asks what minimum access is required. CRU asks what minimum capability is required to perform the specific operation.

Micro-segmentation

ZTA can segment networks, workloads, devices, and applications. CRU makes fine-grained segmentation intrinsic to the CRU layer.

Capabilities

A CRU capability can be narrowly scoped, time-limited, condition-dependent, revocable, auditable, governed, and bound to execution.

05 / CAPABILITY IN CONTEXT

Capability in Context

Identity establishes who is represented; capability establishes what the represented unit may do; governance establishes when and under what conditions that capability may be exercised.

Normal Operating State

Alice may have toy-gun and real-gun capabilities. Bob may have toy-gun and maintenance capabilities. Charlie may have administrative and real-gun capabilities, with bazooka capability conditional. Eve has no authorized capabilities. The point is not identity alone, but the capabilities bound to the governed computational unit.

Elevated Access

When Alice's mission changes, she can receive a narrowly scoped, temporary capability rather than a permanent elevated role. A bazooka capability can be authorized only for a particular mission, under specified conditions, for a defined duration, and while the relevant device state remains compliant.

Normal:
  Toy Gun       ACTIVE
  Real Gun      ACTIVE
  Bazooka       DENIED

Elevated:
  Toy Gun       ACTIVE
  Real Gun      ACTIVE
  Bazooka       CONDITIONALLY ACTIVE
    Mission = X
    Authorization = Y
    Duration = limited
    Device State = compliant

When the governing condition ends, the capability expires or is revoked. Alice's identity did not permanently change; her governed capability set changed.

CIDD: Decision Over Capability in Context

A CIDD decision can evaluate identity, device, mission, current state, requested capability, and governance policy. The result is a decision about the capability that may be exercised under current conditions, rather than simply a trusted/untrusted result.

Identity + Device + Mission + State + Requested Capability + Governance
                              ↓
                             CIDD
                              ↓
                    Capability Decision

Eve Impersonates Alice

Suppose Eve obtains Alice's credentials and presents herself as Alice. An identity claim alone should not automatically confer Alice's governed capabilities. The CRU model distinguishes an identity assertion from the authorized capability composition associated with the governed computational unit.

Elevated Access While Eve Impersonates Alice

Charlie authorizes Alice for temporary bazooka access, and Eve then attempts to impersonate Alice. Eve may possess Alice's username, password, or session artifacts, but those artifacts are not equivalent to the governed capability composition of Alice's CRU.

Alice
  └── Alice-CRU

Eve
  └── Eve-CRU
       └── No Bazooka Capability

This demonstrates why authentication alone is insufficient: the system must establish authority to exercise the capability under the current governance state.

Fine-Grained Segmentation

Traditional segmentation can isolate training, operational, and strategic networks. CRU-level segmentation can go further by treating individual capabilities as governed boundaries, each with identity, authorization, state, execution, and governance conditions.

Alice-CRU
  ├── Toy Gun Capability
  ├── Real Gun Capability
  └── Bazooka Capability

Core Takeaway

Identity establishes who you claim to be. Capability establishes what you are authorized to do. Governance establishes when and under what conditions you may do it. CRU binds these concepts to the computational unit that actually performs the action.

12 / ARCHITECTURAL DISCIPLINE

What CRU Does — and Does Not Claim

CRU is an architectural primitive, not a claim that existing security approaches are obsolete. Zero Trust, IAM, RBAC, ABAC, micro-segmentation, cryptography, and other mechanisms can remain part of a CRU-based system.

CRU does not eliminate segmentation or guarantee security. It provides a more explicit computational boundary for representing, governing, and enforcing capabilities and state.

CRU is an architectural primitive for canonical, portable, cryptographically verifiable, capability-oriented governed computation.
