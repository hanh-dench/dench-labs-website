CRU — Canonical Representation Unit

For decades, digital representations have depended on the systems around them—databases, platforms, repositories, networks, and authorities.

CRU explores a different possibility:

What if the representation itself could become the trusted primitive?

A CRU is designed to create a portable, verifiable digital representation that can remain meaningful across systems and environments—without being defined solely by where it is stored, who hosts it, or which system recognizes it.

Portable. Verifiable. Independent by design.

A new primitive for a distributed world.

Dench Labs is exploring what becomes possible when digital representations can stand on their own.

03 / ZTA VS CRU

Zero Trust secures access. CRU governs capability.

CRU does not reject Zero Trust. Zero Trust establishes powerful security principles; CRU provides a computational architectural primitive in which those principles can become intrinsic to the units performing work.

ZERO TRUST ARCHITECTURE
Identity → Policy → Continuous Verification → Access / Resource

Identity, device posture, context, policy, monitoring, least privilege, and micro-segmentation determine whether access should be granted and maintained.

CRU ARCHITECTURE
Canonical Representation → Integrity → Persistence → Capability Composition → Interoperability

Within the CRU, identity, authorization, execution, state, and governance become coupled to the representation and its capabilities.

Continuous monitoring

ZTA continuously evaluates trust and access conditions. CRU extends this into the lifecycle of the capability itself.

Least privilege

ZTA asks what minimum access is required. CRU asks what minimum capability is required to perform the specific operation.

Micro-segmentation

ZTA can segment networks, workloads, devices, and applications. CRU makes fine-grained segmentation intrinsic to the CRU layer.

Capabilities

A CRU capability can be narrowly scoped, time-limited, condition-dependent, revocable, auditable, governed, and bound to execution.

04 / CAPABILITY ANALOGY

From “can Alice access it?” to “what can Alice do?”

Consider a purely conceptual security analogy involving four actors and three capability levels: a toy gun, a real gun, and a bazooka. The point is not the object—it is the difference between resource access and governed capability.

Changing mission state, risk, device state, identity, location, time, or governance conditions can change the capabilities available to a CRU.

The distinction: Zero Trust can determine whether Alice should access a weapons system. CRU can represent Alice's governed computational unit and determine which capability that unit may exercise, under what conditions, and where that capability may execute.
